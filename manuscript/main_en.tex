\begin{abstract}
Mountain floods damage roads and communication facilities at once, so ground
vehicles can hardly enter the affected areas and UAVs become a key means of
delivery and communication support; severe relief, tight deadlines and the
coupling between the two require a consistent physical model. The paper builds a
``transport scheduling--communication support--resource allocation'' chain solved
in four sequential stages: the transport plan is fixed first and the central stage
constructs a relay schedule meeting coverage, energy and resource constraints.
Problem 1 (DEM-ELBPP) is an energy-aware bin-packing model solved exactly by
set-partitioning dynamic programming, giving an 18-trip plan at 59.24~kWh,
0.69\% below greedy FFD/BFD batching on that instance. Problem 2
(HF-VRPBT) adds heterogeneous fleet routing with time windows and recommends a
20-trip plan at 65.25~kWh satisfying all 31 hard deadlines. Problem 3 introduces
a \emph{multi-hover-point direct-transfer} relay mode: two relay UAVs cover all
11,713 relay-required samples on the 1-s grid (11,645 unique sortie--time pairs)
at a relay energy of 5.43~kWh, whereas the tested single-hover heuristics attain
only 51.0\%, 62.7\% and 82.1\% with one, two and three airframes. On this
instance two relay airframes are scheduled concurrently: two service windows
overlap by 759.8~s with point-cloud centroids 7188.5~m apart, and the best of
397 candidate hover points covers only 2567 of their 3765 relay-required points,
so no single-airframe plan was found. Problem 4 (SC-MAE-RG) compares $K{=}2$ and
$K{=}3$ partitions and
recommends $K{=}2$. A rebuild of the frozen relay plan reproduces the
relay-required count within 0.3\% and the relay energy within 0.1\%; the weakest
relay link keeps 0.38~dB of margin above $-90$~dBm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
Mountain flood disasters strike suddenly and are spatially dispersed, often
destroying roads and communication facilities at the same time. Continuous heavy
rainfall may trigger flash floods, landslides and road collapses, isolating
residents from stable ground transportation for a short period; in the early
post-disaster stage, drinking water, emergency food, medical supplies and
hygiene items must be delivered as soon as possible, while conventional vehicles
can hardly enter the strongly undulating and severely damaged areas. UAVs are
less sensitive to ground-road conditions and can serve as an important
complementary means of emergency transportation.

In July 2026, torrential rainfall brought by typhoon ``Maysak'' caused severe
flooding in Zhenlong Township, Hengzhou, Guangxi. Field reports indicated that
flood water, rockfall and road collapse cut off access from several directions,
leaving the core area temporarily without road traffic and with unstable
communication; the township suffered simultaneous road, power and network
outages and about 8,000 residents were trapped. During the response, food,
drinking water and medicine had to be carried in on foot or dropped by air, and
large UAVs also transported power-repair materials, while temporary
communication and emergency power equipment were deployed to gradually restore
contact. This episode shows that emergency delivery and communication support
must be organized jointly under blocked roads, tight energy budgets and
incomplete information.

From a technical viewpoint, completing delivery depends not only on whether a
UAV can reach the target area, but also on payload, flight distance, climb
height, along-route terrain clearance, energy reservation, deadlines and
equipment turnover. A transportation UAV must also maintain
command, telemetry and hand-over links during climb, cruise and
descent; when fixed facilities are damaged or terrain blocks the
ground gateway, airborne relay UAVs must provide temporary coverage at suitable
positions and time windows. Material delivery under mountain floods therefore
couples transportation, communication support and equipment-resource
coordination.

The four stages studied here are ordered by dependency rather than by
convention. Batching (Problem 1) fixes the indivisible unit loads and their
per-area energy cost before any timing decision is made, because a route
cannot be timed before its payload is known. Routing (Problem 2) then adds
deadlines, airframe concurrency and battery turnover on top of these fixed
batches. Relay scheduling (Problem 3) is meaningful only after the routing
timeline exists, since the relay demand is exactly the set of trajectory points
whose link to the gateway fails. Finally, resource partitioning (Problem 4)
accounts peak concurrency on the joint plan, so it must come last. Each stage
consumes only the outputs of its predecessors, which is what allows one
consistent physical model to run through the chain without re-fitting.
Transportation and communication are physically and temporally coupled---the
relay demand is generated by the transport trajectories---but the chain solves
them sequentially rather than jointly, so no joint optimum is claimed.
Batching and routing form an input-generating module, resource partitioning is
an extension analysis, and relay scheduling under the fixed transport plan is
the core of this paper.

\subsection{Related Work}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
Relay deployment under terrain blockage is critical for link quality:
\cite{cuu2026} showed that even a single well-placed relay can significantly
improve link reliability, and in emergency logistics \cite{wu2024} revealed a
timeliness--energy trade-off.
Cellular-connected delivery and joint UAV--ground routing have since been
studied extensively: trajectory optimization for cellular-connected fleets
\cite{cao2024}, joint delivery routing of UAVs and ground vehicles from an
urban-logistics perspective \cite{wuXu2026} and under regional restrictions
\cite{wu2026}, and multi-objective design of UAV transportation networks
\cite{xia2019}. In cooperative mountain logistics, \cite{sun2026} planned
drone--vehicle orchard transport, \cite{dong2026} developed three-dimensional
path planning and scheduling for urban emergency UAVs under typhoon disasters,
and \cite{wan2024} designed dedicated flood search-and-rescue UAVs. On the
communication side, \cite{sabuncu2024} optimized drone-to-everything links for
medical services and \cite{huang2024} improved UAV-assisted downlink
transmission, while \cite{zhang2021} studied self-triggered cooperative control
of tethered multi-UAV transportation under low communication, and \cite{li2026}
surveyed UAV--UGV collaborative sensing for low-altitude transportation.
Heterogeneous multi-UAV swarms \cite{ma2020} and two-stage UAV facility location
and scheduling with objective-robustness optimization \cite{guo2026} are also
closely related. Most of these studies address either the transportation or the
communication side: routing works rarely enforce continuous link coverage, while
communication works rarely account for payload, deadline and battery-turnover
limits. A few come closer: the siting model of \cite{guo2026} decides where
ground facilities stand but not how airborne links are maintained during
delivery; the sensing survey of \cite{li2026} catalogs collaboration patterns
without a transport--communication optimizer; \cite{yanmaz2023} compares joint
and decoupled multi-UAV path planning for search and rescue; and \cite{soya2025}
designs delivery-UAV routes that reserve multi-hop relays. The closest
precedent is the HDNF preprint \cite{huang2026hdnf} (an arXiv preprint
submitted to IEEE, not yet peer reviewed), which jointly optimizes delivery
task assignment, the 3-D deployment of hovering UAV base stations and
C2-aware delivery path planning; its link model is a probabilistic
elevation-angle LoS channel, its base stations hold fixed positions, and its
objective trades C2 reliability against flight energy and deployment cost in
synthetic simulations. These works, however, treat the relay as a static
route constraint or a fixed-position asset rather than a scheduled resource:
none decides the relay hover sequence, its transfer flights and link
re-establishment, nor the turnover of a shared energy-component inventory
over the transport timeline, and none verifies the links against terrain.
The gap addressed here is therefore the sequential,
physically consistent chain in which transport batching and routing are fixed
first and the multi-hover-point relay schedule is then constructed under
coverage, energy and resource constraints. Table~\ref{tab:rw} positions these
works against the three capabilities the coupled problem requires simultaneously:
continuous link coverage (L), payload/energy feasibility (P) and
battery/component turnover (T).

\subsection{Contributions}
The physical rules used throughout---nonlinear equivalent range, DEM-derived
cruise altitude, climb energy, two-stage charging, link budget and fleet
inventory---are prescribed by the relief scenario and adopted as given, not
claimed as new. Within that scope:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Multi-hover-point relay scheduling (MHPRS), the core
contribution.} We formalize a mode in which one relay UAV serves several hover
points per take-off/return cycle, state the mode-dominance proposition (the
single-hover feasible set is a subset) and characterize, for the test instance,
the concurrent service windows that motivate the two-airframe plan. Two relay
UAVs cover all
11,713 sampled relay-required points on the 1-s grid, against 51.0\%, 62.7\% and
82.1\% for the tested single-hover heuristics with one, two and three airframes.
\item \textbf{A consistent, independently verified implementation.} Each
headline number is recomputed from the frozen outputs by a separate script
(coverage within $0.3\%$, relay energy within $0.1\%$); this is a reproducibility
contribution, not a new physical law, and the sequential chain claims no joint
optimum.
\item \textbf{An energy-feasibility discretization for batching.} The batching
subproblem itself is solved by a standard subset enumeration plus
set-partitioning DP; the added element is the reformulation of the continuous
energy constraint into feasible pair loads, giving a verified benchmark (18
trips, 59.24\,kWh, 0.69\% below FFD/BFD); no DP novelty is claimed.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}\label{sec:sys}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System Overview}
The temporary dispatch center $O_{01}$ provides ground control, the communication
gateway (G01, antenna 20 m above ground), and battery/energy-component
management. Three types of transportation UAVs (A/B/C) execute round trips under
a return-safety margin $\rho=20\%$, delivering box by box to 15 service areas subject to
payload, volume and energy constraints. Shadowed back-slope segments are
relayed by two relay UAVs using multi-hover-point direct transfer, maintaining
end-to-end connectivity through a ``transport--relay--G01'' two-hop link (both
directions $\ge -90$ dBm). Relay UAVs return to base to swap energy components
after each take-off/service/return cycle. Fig.~\ref{fig:system} summarizes the
overall architecture.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\linewidth]{fig_system.pdf}
\caption{UAV transportation--communication collaborative system architecture.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Model assumptions.} Five assumptions define the scope, each trading
some realism for reproducibility. (A1)~Terrain and link state are static within
each one-second sample, matching the verification grid; shorter outages are
therefore outside the coverage guarantee. (A2)~Terrain occlusion is binary
with a fixed 10\,dB loss rather than a diffraction model, which makes the
line-of-sight test deterministic and fully reproducible but less radio-faithful.
(A3)~Wind, precipitation and equipment faults are excluded, so plans are valid
for a single deterministic post-disaster scenario. (A4)~Demand (box masses,
volumes and deadlines) is known and fixed; stochastic or rolling demand is left
to future work. (A5)~Relay demand is computed from the planned transport
trajectories, so the communication problem is conditional on the Problem-2
schedule; this preserves the stage ordering but excludes replanning after
unplanned detours.

\subsection{Shared Physical Model (used by all stages)}
\subsubsection{Distance and cruise altitude}
The great-circle distance between $O_{01}$ and service area $S_j$ uses the
Haversine formula:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{Oj}&=2R\arctan\!\sqrt{\frac{a}{1-a}},\\
a&=\sin^{2}\!\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2}
+\cos\varphi_{1}\cos\varphi_{2}\sin^{2}\!\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\varphi,\Delta\lambda$ are latitude/longitude differences and $R$ is
the Earth radius. The cruise altitude along a segment takes the DEM elevation by
the nearest-pixel rule plus 50\,m:
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{cruise}}_{(j)}=h^{\max}_{(j)}+50 .
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Trip energy and return-safety margin}
The total round-trip energy consists of four parts (outbound level, return
level, outbound climb, return climb):
\begin{equation}
E^{\mathrm{total}}_{(i,j)}(q)=E^{\mathrm{go}}_{\mathrm{hor}}(q)+E^{\mathrm{ret}}_{\mathrm{hor}}(0)
+E^{\mathrm{go}}_{\mathrm{climb}}(q)+E^{\mathrm{ret}}_{\mathrm{climb}},
\end{equation}
with outbound payload $q$ and empty return. The level-flight parts follow a
nonlinear equivalent-range law: with $L_0$ the empty and $L_F$ the full-load
standard range and $Q$ the rated payload, the equivalent range at payload $q$
is
\begin{align}
L_g(q)&=L_0-(L_0-L_F)\Big(\frac{q}{Q}\Big)^{3/2},\\
E^{\mathrm{go}}_{\mathrm{hor}}(q)&=E^{\mathrm{bat}}\frac{d}{L_g(q)},\qquad
E^{\mathrm{ret}}_{\mathrm{hor}}(0)=E^{\mathrm{bat}}\frac{d}{L_0},
\end{align}
so that loading reduces range superlinearly. The climb parts are potential
energy terms with efficiency $\eta_c=0.72$ and no descent recovery:
\begin{align}
E^{\mathrm{go}}_{\mathrm{climb}}(q)&=\frac{g(m_0+q)(H-h_O)}{\eta_c\cdot3.6\times10^{6}},\\
E^{\mathrm{ret}}_{\mathrm{climb}}&=\frac{g\,m_0(H-h_S-30)}{\eta_c\cdot3.6\times10^{6}},
\end{align}
where $m_0$ is the curb mass, $H$ the cruise altitude, $h_O$ the depot
elevation, $h_S$ the service-area elevation and the $30$\,m offset is the
hand-over height above the delivery point; energies are in kWh. The usable
energy with margin $\rho$ is
\begin{equation}
E^{\mathrm{avail}}_{(i)}=E^{\mathrm{bat}}_{(i)}\cdot(1-\rho),
\qquad
E^{\mathrm{total}}_{(i,j)}(m^{\mathrm{safe}}_{(i,j)})\le E^{\mathrm{avail}}_{(i)} ,
\end{equation}
from which the maximum safe payload $m^{\mathrm{safe}}_{(i,j)}$ is obtained by
binary search. The corresponding trip time combines vertical and horizontal
flight with ground handling,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
t_{\mathrm{trip}}={}&(2H-h_O-h_S-30)\Big(\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{up}}}+\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{dn}}}\Big)
+\frac{2d}{v}\\
&+t_{\mathrm{prep}}+t^{0}_{\mathrm{hand}}
+n\,(t_{\mathrm{load}}+t^{1}_{\mathrm{hand}}),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of boxes on the trip, $v_{\mathrm{up}},v_{\mathrm{dn}}$
are the climb and descent speeds and the last terms are the preparation,
loading and hand-over times of Table~\ref{tab:platform}.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Overview of the Unified Framework}
We establish a ``transport--communication--resource'' collaborative
optimization chain: with 30\,m DEM terrain, 15 service areas, 80 indivisible
boxes and heterogeneous UAV fleets plus link parameters as unified inputs, we
successively build DEM-ELBPP (Problem 1), HF-VRPBT (Problem 2), MHPRS (Problem
3) and SC-MAE-RG (Problem 4). The four layers introduce return-safety margin and
batching, deadline concurrency and battery turnover, full-process continuous
communication, and partitioning resource allocation, respectively; the overall
technical route is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:route}.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\linewidth]{fig_route.pdf}
\caption{Overall technical route of the proposed framework.}
\label{fig:route}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Problem 1: DEM-Driven Energy-Aware Bin Packing (DEM-ELBPP)}
For the box set $B_j$ at service area $S_j$ ($|B_j|=n_j$), batching is modeled as
an energy-constrained hierarchical bin packing: each trip $\pi_k$ loads a nonempty
subset of $B_j$ executed by type $\mu_k\in\{A,B,C\}$, subject to payload, volume
and energy constraints. Among the three objectives (trip count, total energy,
cumulative operating time) we adopt \emph{lexicographic} optimization instead of a
weighted sum, because the three have different dimensions and arbitrary weights
would change the optimum subjectively:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\text{lex}}\Big(N_{\mathrm{trips}},\ \sum_{k}E_{k},\ T_{\mathrm{total}}\Big)
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{align}
&\bigcup_{k}\pi_k=B_j,\qquad \pi_k\cap\pi_{k'}=\varnothing\ (k\ne k'),\\
&\sum_{n\in\pi_k}m_{n}\le m^{\mathrm{safe}}_{(i,j)},\qquad
\sum_{n\in\pi_k}V_{n}\le V^{\max}_{(i)},\\
&\mu_k\in\{A,B,C\},\quad \pi_k\subseteq B_j\ \text{(single area, no crossing).}
\end{align}
The key idea is to first back-solve the safe payload from the energy equation and
then perform set partitioning under the payload--volume--energy triple constraint,
discretizing the continuous energy constraint into bin-packing feasibility.

Candidate batches are enumerated per area (the largest area holds $n_j=15$
boxes, so at most $2^{15}-1=32767$ subsets), and the exact solution uses
set-partitioning dynamic programming:
\begin{equation}
dp[\text{mask}\cup\pi]=\min\Big(dp[\text{mask}\cup\pi],\ dp[\text{mask}]\oplus(\pi)\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\oplus$ is vector addition and triples are compared lexicographically;
the optimal batch sequence is then recovered by backtracking the predecessor
chain. Two implementation facts bound the computational cost. First, the 45
machine--area safe payloads are precomputed once by a 60-iteration binary
search on the energy equation, so the DP never evaluates the continuous energy
model. Second, because each area holds at most fifteen boxes, the per-area
candidate count is at most $2^{15}-1=32767$ subsets before the
payload--volume--energy filter and far fewer after it, and the DP state space
is the $2^{15}$ masks of that area; mixed-type batching within an area is
allowed, so the DP chooses the cheapest machine per candidate subset rather
than fixing the type in advance. The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:q1}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Exact batching (subset enumeration + set-partitioning DP)}
\label{alg:q1}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{input} box sets $B_j$, UAV parameters, DEM, margin $\rho$
\STATE binary-search $m^{\mathrm{safe}}_{(i,j)}$ for each machine/area
\STATE enumerate feasible subsets per area, filter by payload/volume/energy $\to$ candidate batches $C_j$
\STATE init $dp[0]=(0,0,0)$, others $\infty$; relax candidate batches lexicographically
\STATE backtrack to output the optimal batching plan
\STATE scan $\rho=20\%\sim40\%$ and repeat for sensitivity
\STATE \textbf{output} optimal batching plan, per-area machine/energy, margin sensitivity
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Problem 2: Heterogeneous Fleet Routing with Battery and Time Windows (HF-VRPBT)}
Problem 2 relaxes the single-area structure and adds resource and time-window
constraints: each trip may visit one or more service areas before returning.
The decisions jointly determine box batching, visit sequence, machine type
$\mu_k$, the executing airframe, the shared battery and the trip start time.
With $B$ the 80 boxes,
$B^{\mathrm{hard}}$ the 31 hard-deadline boxes and $B^{\mathrm{soft}}$ the rest,
the constraints enforce one delivery per box, per-trip payload and volume
limits, segment-wise residual payload, trip energy, return SOC $\ge\rho$, hard
deadlines $t_b\le d_b$ for all $b\in B^{\mathrm{hard}}$, airframe and battery
mutual exclusion, and recharge to $100\%$ before battery reuse. Formally, with
$x_{kb}\in\{0,1\}$ marking the assignment of box $b$ to trip $k$, $[s_k,r_k]$
the interval trip $k$ occupies its airframe ($s_k$: preparation start, $r_k$:
return) and $[s_k,c_k]$ the interval its battery is occupied
($c_k=r_k+T_{\mathrm{chg}}(\mathrm{SOC}_k)$: fully recharged), the scheduler
accepts a plan only if
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\textstyle\sum_{k}x_{kb}=1\ \ (b\in B),\qquad
\sum_{b}x_{kb}m_{b}\le m^{\mathrm{safe}}_{(\mu_k)},\\
&\textstyle\sum_{b}x_{kb}V_{b}\le V^{\max}_{(\mu_k)},\qquad
t_{b}\le d_{b}\ \ (b\in B^{\mathrm{hard}}),\\
&\mathrm{SOC}_{k}=1-E_{k}/E^{\mathrm{bat}}_{(\mu_k)}\ge\rho,\\
&[s_k,r_k]\cap[s_{k'},r_{k'}]=\varnothing\ \ \text{(same airframe),}\\
&[s_k,c_k]\cap[s_{k'},c_{k'}]=\varnothing\ \ \text{(same battery),}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ trips sharing an airframe or a battery never overlap, and a battery is
reusable only after a full recharge, which takes
$T_{\mathrm{chg}}(\mathrm{SOC})=T^{\mathrm{full}}_{(\mu)}\,[0.35+0.65\,(0.9-\mathrm{SOC})/0.9]$\,s
under the two-stage charging rule ($T^{\mathrm{full}}=1800/2400/3000$\,s for
A/B/C; all return SOCs in the computed plans lie below $0.9$). Along the visit
sequence $\pi_k$ the residual payload is recursive,
$\Lambda_{k,j}=\Lambda_{k,j-1}-\sum_{b:\mathrm{pos}(b)=j}m_b$,
$\Lambda_{k,0}=\sum_b x_{kb}m_b$: each leg carries exactly the boxes still
undelivered; multi-area sequences are restricted to type C, and the delivery
instant $t_b$ follows the cumulative leg-time recursion
(climb--cruise--descend per leg at the DEM-checked cruise altitude). Four
objectives---timeliness, makespan $C_{\max}$ (latest return), transport energy
and trip count---are compared in a non-dominated sense, with the soft delay
$L_{\mathrm{soft}}=\sum_{b\in B^{\mathrm{soft}}}\max(0,t_b-d_b)$ measuring the
lateness of the remaining boxes. Since the full trip-routing space is
intractable, we enumerate a restricted candidate set (whole-area trips inherited
from Problem 1, split first-batch tasks and short cross-area tours) and apply a
deterministic urgency-first greedy schedule that inserts trips by nearest hard
deadline. Inherited whole-area trips
preserve the energy-optimality of the Problem-1 batches, split first-batch tasks
decouple urgent first deliveries from the residual load so that hard deadlines
are met before the full batch is ready, and short cross-area tours absorb small
residual loads that would otherwise each cost a dedicated round trip; each
insertion recomputes the occupancies of the chosen airframe and battery and
rejects placements violating the conditions above. The search evaluates 57
candidate plans and yields five non-dominated plans (Table~\ref{tab:pareto}):
19 trips ($66.30$\,kWh), the recommended 20-trip plan ($65.25$\,kWh,
$C_{\max}=2.72$\,h, soft delay $137.6$\,min), two further 20-trip plans that
trade a longer makespan for smaller soft delay, and 21 trips ($66.00$\,kWh);
the recommended 20-trip plan
dominates on energy and completion time, uses all eight airframes and satisfies
all 31 hard-deadline boxes.

\subsection{Problem 3: Task-Driven Multi-Hover-Point Relay Scheduling (MHPRS)}
\label{sec:mhprs}
\subsubsection{Link feasibility}
Link feasibility uses a free-space path-loss model with a binary DEM
line-of-sight occlusion test (10\,dB occlusion loss when blocked):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P_r=\ &P_t+G_t+G_r-32.45-20\log_{10}f-20\log_{10}d\\
&-L_{\mathrm{obs}}-L_{\mathrm{sys}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $L_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\{0,10\}$\,dB and $L_{\mathrm{sys}}=3$\,dB; $f$ is
in MHz, the three-dimensional slant range $d$ is in km, powers are in dBm
and antenna gains in dBi. Both
directions (transport$\leftrightarrow$relay and relay$\leftrightarrow$G01) must
satisfy $P_r\ge P_{\mathrm{thr}}=-90$\,dBm. The relay platform is a
$23.5$\,kg multi-rotor with a $3.2$\,kWh swappable energy component: it cruises
at $1.15$\,kW, and draws a flat $1.05+0.05$\,kW over each service window
($1.05$\,kW platform hover plus $0.05$\,kW communication payload), recharges in
$T_{\mathrm{chg}}=500+1300\,(1-\mathrm{SOC})$\,s under the two-stage charging
rule ($1800$\,s from a fully depleted component), and must land with
$\mathrm{SOC}\ge0.20$; six components circulate between the two airframes and
the chargers.

\subsubsection{Multi-hover-point relay scheduling (MHPRS)}
\textbf{Definition (MHPRS).} Given a set of relay-required time intervals and a
set of candidate hover points, a \emph{relay sortie} is a take-off/service/return
cycle of one relay UAV serving an ordered sequence of hover points connected by
direct-transfer flights; MHPRS is to cover all relay-required points by the
minimum-cost set of sorties under link-budget, hover-height, resource-exclusion,
return-SOC and component-inventory constraints.
Classical relay placement installs fixed relays so that a static set of
terminals becomes connected and optimizes positions only. MHPRS differs in three
respects: (i) the demand is \emph{time-varying}, since relay-required points
appear and disappear as the transport trips progress, so coverage is
spatio-temporal rather than purely spatial; (ii) one airframe may serve an
\emph{ordered sequence} of hover points within one sortie, and the transfer
flight and link re-establishment between consecutive points consume time and
energy that enter the constraints; and (iii) only two airframes share six energy
components, so hover-height, energy and component-turnover limits bind the
solution. Formally, the constraints comprise full-process coverage (each
transport point is either directly connected to G01 or lies in some sortie's
service interval with both hops above threshold), relay-before-task
$s_c\le\min\{t_p\}$, bidirectional link budget
$P_r\ge P_{\mathrm{thr}}$, hover-height limit
$h_c\le h^{\mathrm{terr}}_c+300$\,m, resource exclusion, return SOC $\ge0.20$,
component inventory (in-flight plus charging $\le6$, reusable only when fully
charged), coverage rate
$\ge\eta_{\mathrm{target}}$, and the transfer--link-establish timing between
consecutive segments; the coverage rate counts verified relay-required
trajectory points after removing duplicate boundary samples between consecutive
segments.

The solution procedure mirrors this structure: relay-required points are
clustered into service intervals by greater-than-$400$\,s gaps; candidate hover
points are generated on the $0.01^\circ$ DEM grid within $300$\,m above ground
and $12$\,km of the depot, keeping only points from which every relay-required
point of a segment passes the link budget; a greedy pass selects the fewest
candidates per interval, splitting at the median time when a segment remains
uncovered, and the resulting adjacent segments are packed into sorties under the
component-inventory and return-SOC constraints with earliest-available assignment
of the two airframes.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (mode dominance, elementary).} Let $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{SH}}$ be
the set of coverage plans feasible when every sortie is restricted to a single
hover point, and $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{MH}}$ the set feasible under MHPRS with
ordered hover-point chains. Then
$\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{SH}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{MH}}$; consequently,
for any coverage target $\eta$, (i) if $\eta$ is attainable at all by the
single-hover mode with a given fleet, it is attainable by MHPRS with the same
fleet, and (ii) the minimum energy required under MHPRS is no greater than
under the single-hover mode. \emph{Proof.} A single-hover sortie is the
length-one special case of an MHPRS chain: relabeling every single-hover
sortie as a length-one chain adds no transfer flight, and all other
constraints (link budget, hover height, resource exclusion, return SOC,
component inventory) are identical, so the relabeled plan remains feasible at
the same energy. Minimizing a common cost over a superset of feasible plans
cannot raise the optimum.\hfill $\square$

\noindent
\emph{Empirical gap.} The tested single-hover heuristics attain only
$51.0\%$, $62.7\%$ and $82.1\%$ with one, two and three airframes, whereas
MHPRS covers all 11,713 sampled relay-required points with four sorties.

\textbf{Observation 1 (relay concurrency on the test instance).} In the
evaluated candidate pool, a single relay airframe is not sufficient for the test
instance. Two first-segment service windows overlap, $[751.8,1512.6]$\,s and
$[752.8,1712.7]$\,s, sharing $759.8$\,s of time, while their point-cloud
centroids lie $7188.5$\,m apart. The union of their relay-required points
contains $3765$ samples; of the $397$ enumerated candidate hover points the best
covers $2567$, and the single-airframe schedule search returns no feasible plan.
The two-airframe plan is therefore used. This requirement is empirical: it rests
on exhaustive enumeration over the evaluated candidate pool and on the failure
of the single-airframe search, not on a geometric lower bound, because an MHPRS
airframe may relocate between hover points.

The combined stage-3 objective is
\begin{align}
\min Z &= E_{\mathrm{trans}}+E_{\mathrm{relay}}, \notag\\
E_{\mathrm{relay}} &= \sum_{c}\!\Big(E^{\mathrm{fly}}_{c}
+(P_{\mathrm{hover}}+P_{\mathrm{comm}})\tfrac{e_c-s_c}{3600}
+P_{\mathrm{hover}}\tfrac{w_c}{3600}\Big),
\end{align}
where $w_c$ is the accumulated hovering wait time within sortie $c$ and
$E^{\mathrm{fly}}_{c}$ is the outbound/transfer/return flight energy. The
three terms are computed over mutually disjoint intervals of the sortie
timeline: inter-segment transfer time is charged to $E^{\mathrm{fly}}_{c}$
and excluded from the served-hover window $[s_c,e_c]$, so no energy is
double-counted. The stage-3 objective is subject to
the constraint set listed in the MHPRS definition above. The stage-3 solver is a
greedy construction over the candidate hover points, so the returned schedule
satisfies the constraints above but minimizes neither the relay energy nor the
sortie count in a proven sense; Observation~1 supports the two-airframe plan
only within the evaluated candidate pool. The resulting
multi-hover-point direct-transfer mode, together with its coverage advantage
over the single-hover baseline, is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:mhprs} and
summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:q3}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Task-driven multi-hover-point relay assignment (MHPRS)}
\label{alg:q3}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{input} Problem-2 schedule, DEM, link params, 2 relay UAVs, 6 components
\STATE sample trajectories per second, mark relay-required points
\STATE cluster relay-required points by time ($>$400\,s gap) into service intervals
\STATE generate candidate hover points (DEM-covered, $\le$300\,m, $\le$12\,km)
\STATE greedy select points per cluster; split by median time if uncovered
\STATE pack adjacent segments into sorties (multi-hover direct transfer); earliest-available assignment
\STATE check component turnover (two-stage charging) and return SOC
\STATE full verification by per-second sampling and boundary checks
\STATE \textbf{output} relay schedule: hover-point chains, service windows, verified coverage and per-sortie SOC
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Problem 4: Partition-Based Resource Accounting (SC-MAE-RG)}
Problem 4 takes the Problem-3 joint plan as input and groups the 15 service
areas into $K\in\{2,3\}$ task groups, subject to each area belonging to exactly
one group, each group containing at least three areas (an explicit operational
grouping assumption: one- or two-area groups degenerate the grouping into
per-area accounting; the one-area variant is reported in the benchmark for
completeness), and any areas that share a transport trip being placed in the
same group. Each group independently accounts
for the eight resource classes (A/B/C airframes, A/B/C batteries, relay airframes
and energy components) at their peak concurrency, because resources may not be
reassigned across groups. For group $P_i$ and resource class $q$ the peak
concurrent demand is
$N(P_i,q)=\max_\tau\sum_{u:S_u\cap P_i\ne\varnothing}\mathbf{1}\{\tau\in[a_u,b_u],\,\mathrm{type}(u)=q\}$,
from which the configuration scale $D=\sum_i\sum_q N(P_i,q)$, the inventory gap
$G=\sum_q\max(0,\sum_i N(P_i,q)-I_q)$, the cross-group relay count, the
replication total and the inter-group balance index $J$ follow; both $D$ and
$G$ sum the eight resource classes with equal weight, so they act as
inventory-count proxies rather than cost-weighted measures. Candidate
partitions are enumerated from $80$ $K$-means seeds on standardized geographic
coordinates (with the S008--S011 shared-sortie coupling enforced), two
hand-built skeletons and the relay-service-induced split, and ranked
lexicographically by (unique cross-group relays, extra cross-group replicas,
$D$, $G$, $J$); the winner is the best partition within this pool, not a
certified optimum over all partitions. The balance index is the mean
coefficient of variation across six workload dimensions (boxes, mass, sorties,
energy, busy duration and relay count),
\begin{equation}
J(\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{\ell=1}^{6}\frac{\sigma_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})}
{\mu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})},
\end{equation}
so that $J{=}0$ marks a perfectly even split and larger values indicate
workload concentration. On the test instance the recommended $K{=}2$ partition
$[11,4]$ attains $D{=}31$ and $G{=}5$, against $D{=}38$ and $G{=}10$ for
$K{=}3$ $[7,4,4]$, so $K{=}2$ is preferred (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp} details the
per-class gaps and the zero-replication control). An
entropy-weighted TOPSIS ranking (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}) identifies S001 as the
most delivery-pressured area, consistent with the heavy load carried by the
larger group.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}

single-hover baseline of Fig.~\ref{fig:mhprs}(b) is evaluated under exactly
the same link budget, airframe inventory, per-sortie energy model, return-SOC
floor and two-stage charging rule as MHPRS, so the coverage gap reflects the
mode under the tested implementations rather than more generous resources; the
baseline is a one-hover-per-sortie heuristic, not a proven optimum of that mode.

The resource-directed enumeration is benchmarked against generic unsupervised
clustering: $K$-means ($100$ restarts) and Ward agglomerative clustering on
standardized coordinates (geo) and on coordinates plus
boxes--mass--volume--first-batch demand (geo+demand). Each clustering output
receives the identical S008--S011 feasibility repair (shared sortie T18);
all partitions are scored by the same peak-concurrency accounting and rows
violating the three-area rule are marked
$^{\dagger}$ and excluded from adoption.
Table~\ref{tab:benchq4} reports all partitions. For $K{=}2$,
SC-MAE-RG attains the smallest scale and gap ($31/5$) among the evaluated
partitions satisfying the shared-sortie coupling and the three-area rule,
against $36/9$ and $37/9$ for the geo
clusterings, and $38/10$ for $K{=}3$ against $43/15$ and $40/12$. The geo
clusterings obtain a smaller (better) balance index $J$ but at the price of more
cross-group relay sorties and replicas, so the comparison is a trade-off among
resource scale, inventory gap and cross-group relay redundancy rather than a
strict dominance. The demand-feature variants attain a lower $D/G$
($29/4$ for $K{=}2$) by isolating
S001 (154\,kg, $1.9\times$ the next-largest area) into a
singleton group ($1{+}14$; $1{+}7{+}7$ and $1{+}5{+}9$ for
$K{=}3$). These outputs satisfy the shared-sortie coupling---T18, the only
multi-area sortie, links S011 and S008, which remain together, and S001's
trips are single-area---so they are feasible under the one-area rule and
attain the smallest $D/G$ among the evaluated candidates; they are inadmissible
only under the three-area rule
and are reported for completeness rather than adopted. Generic clustering thus
minimizes feature dispersion without
perceiving peak concurrency or the non-splittable shared-sortie coupling.

\subsection{Discussion}
The partitioning stage converts the coupled schedule into an actionable
decision: the recommended $K{=}2$ plan has a quantified gap of five resource
units (two B-type airframes, two B-type batteries and one relay airframe). The
outputs map onto field operations: executable per-trip load and route sheets,
four relay sorties over seven service windows verified point-by-point at 1-s
resolution, DEM cruise altitudes that keep the prescribed clearance, and a
procurement list naming the five missing units; the zero-replication control
shows cross-group relay sharing, not vehicle count, to be binding.

Two threats to validity deserve emphasis. First, the energy model is validated
only against frozen computational artifacts: the reconstruction reproduces the
relay-required count within 0.3\% and the relay energy within 0.1\%, but the
hover points of two of the seven service windows are re-derived from the DEM
grid rather than read from the
frozen log, so this is a reconstruction check rather than an independent flight
measurement, and no hardware flight test has been performed; wind, payload-drag
and battery-aging effects could shift the absolute figures. The reconstructed
timeline also assumes an aggregate $37$\,s climb allowance for A/B departures
(those samples remain directly connected), a raised corridor on S007 legs, and a
$6$\,m clearance with bilinear interpolation in the direct-link test; the
relay-coverage test uses the raw DEM profile, so its 100\% figure is tied to the
reconstruction convention; at $6$\,m one reconstructed point leaves a service
window uncovered, while relocating it by $256$\,m restores full coverage with
$582$\,s of arrival slack, return state of charge $0.41$ and sortie energy
$1.2\%$ above the frozen value. Second, relay
demand is identified on a five-second search grid, which underestimates the
relay-required population relative to the one-second verification grid; the
11,713-sample coverage claim therefore holds only on the one-second grid under
the stated link model, and the candidate search may miss short-duration
blockage events. Further limitations are that link feasibility is judged by a
binary DEM line-of-sight test with a fixed occlusion loss, and that the
environment is deterministic and does not model weather or equipment faults. For
Problem 2, the joint batching--routing--scheduling optimum is not searched, so
the $65.25$\,kWh main plan remains the best of the enumerated candidate
structures under the urgency-first scheduler rather than a proven joint optimum.
Because the parameters are data-driven, the chain transfers to other mountainous
settings, but the reported fleet configuration and coverage figures are
instance-specific.

Transferring the chain to a new site requires re-running rather than
re-designing: recompute the safe-payload table from a regional DEM, enter the
available fleet into the parameter tables, re-identify the relay-required
population, and run the four stages in order; the tour enumeration and the
per-second verification are embarrassingly parallel.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
This paper built a four-stage ``transport scheduling--communication
support--resource allocation'' framework that runs on one consistent physical
model; its core stage constructs, once the delivery plan is fixed, a relay
schedule that satisfies coverage, energy and resource constraints. The exact
batching model returns an 18-trip plan at 59.24\,kWh with a verified optimality
benchmark; the heterogeneous routing model delivers all 80 boxes, satisfies all
31 hard deadlines and recommends a 20-trip plan at 65.25\,kWh; and the relay
stage covers all 11,713 sampled relay-required points with two airframes and four
sorties at 5.43\,kWh (joint 70.68\,kWh). The partitioning stage then quantifies
the resource gap and produces an explicit augmentation list. The sensitivity
studies delineate the envelope: batching tolerates return margins up to $35\%$
(failing at $40\%$), relay demand is governed by the receiving threshold rather
than the terrain clearance, and only the multi-hover mode reaches full coverage
on the 1-s grid with two airframes.

Further work should replace the one-second grid with event-driven
continuous-time link checking, add wind, weather, stochastic demand and
multi-depot sharing, couple scheduling with batching in one joint model, and
report acceptance rates on end-to-end geographic families.
